### Visualization 1

Software: I used Python. pandas handled the data cleaning, and matplotlib built the graphic. I replicated it in R's ggplot too.

Audience: Ontario educators, administrators, policymakers, and parents who could use insights without analyzing a 4,900-row spreadsheet.

Message: There is a severe bottleneck in middle-elementary math. Students are doing okay in Grade 3, but their proficiency notably decreases by Grade 6.

Design Choices: I used a slope chart/slope graph instead of standard bars because slopes communicate the change in an intuitive manner.

Decluttering: I stripped out the outer borders, standard tick marks, and detached legends to maximize the data-to-ink ratio.

Color: I pushed the individual school boards into the background using a faint grey, and used the UofT teal blue to make the provincial average pop.

Direct Labeling: I put the numbers right next to the data points so viewers don't have to bounce their eyes between the chart and a legend.

Reproducibility: Because the chart is generated by a Python script, it is reproducible. Anyone can download the dataset, run the code, and get the same image.

Accessibility: I used high contrast (dark text/bold blue on off-white). I didn't rely solely on the visual slope to show the result. I added an explicit text annotation too so anyone can grasp the main point with ease.

Impacted Communities: The students navigating the curriculum, the Grade 6 teachers who might face unfair scrutiny, and the school boards performing below the red line.

Included vs. Excluded: To tell a clear story, I hand picked data from the dataset. I ignored most variables. I only kept Grade 3 and 6 math scores. I also excluded small boards (under 20 schools) to avoid too many lines.

Underwater Labour: The chart looks simple, but it relies on hidden work from several stakeholders. This includes the kids taking the EQAO tests, the teachers administering them, the government clerks who compiled the data, and the open-source developers who built the Python libraries I used for free.

### Visualization 2

Software: I used Python. pandas was used for data cleaning, and matplotlib was used for the graphic. I replicated it in R's ggplot2 too.

Audience: Board trustees, superintendents, and policymakers who just want the bottom line on the biggest districts without staring at a spreadsheet.

The Message: The Grade 3 to 6 math drop-off happens everywhere, but the difference and absolute values vary. York Region holds it to an 8% drop, while Durham tanks by nearly 20%.

Design Choices: I used a horizontal dumbbell plot. Much easier to read 10 long board names on the y-axis than cramming them onto the x-axis.

Sorting: Sorted the y-axis by Grade 3 starting scores so the chart looks organized.

Decluttering: Stripped out all the border boxes, y-axis grid lines, and axis titles. Placed the legend directly in the subtitle to save space.

Reproducibility: It’s fully scripted in python and R. Assuming correct paths and the like, it can run the code, and spit out the exact same PNG every time. I avoided doing manual dragging, dropping, etc.

Accessibility: I didn't rely on people having to visually guess the gap by tracing lines down to the x-axis. I calculated the drop percentage and slapped it right in the middle of the connecting line. High contrast colors, big fonts, minimal cognitive load.

Impacted Communities: The boards on this list—especially Durham, Peel, and Dufferin-Peel, who might lose reputation (e.g., frustrated parents) for having the biggest drop-offs. Also, potentially Grade 6 teachers taking the blame for structural curriculum issues.

Included vs. Excluded: I only kept the top 10 out of 83 school boards. Trying to plot all 83 on one chart was illegible. I also ignored all reading, writing, and demographic data to keep the focus strictly on math.

Underwater Labour: The kids taking the actual EQAO tests, the teachers administering them, the data clerks compiling the EQAO dataset, and the open-source devs who maintain matplotlib and ggplot2.
